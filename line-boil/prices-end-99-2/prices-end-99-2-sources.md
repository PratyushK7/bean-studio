# Sources: prices-end-99-2

| Claim in the video | Source | How far checked |
|---|---|---|
| People read numbers left to right and weigh the leftmost digit most (the left-digit effect); a price like 3.99 can be perceived as lower than 4.00 by more than one cent | Thomas & Morwitz (2005), "Penny Wise and Pound Foolish: The Left Digit Effect in Price Cognition", Journal of Consumer Research. https://phys.org/news/2005-06-consumers-react-prices.html | abstract and secondary report |
| Price endings can shift which product people pick | Manning & Sprott (2009), "Price Endings, Left-Digit Effects, and Choice", Journal of Consumer Research 36(2). https://econbiz.de/Record/price-endings-left-hyphen-digit-effects-and-choice-manning-kenneth/10009323850 | abstract only |
| Field experiments on retail sales with 9 price endings | Anderson & Simester (2003), "Effects of $9 Price Endings on Retail Sales: Evidence from Field Experiments", Quantitative Marketing and Economics 1(1) | abstract and secondary report |
| Left-digit bias shows up in other big purchases (used cars at 10,000-mile odometer marks) | Lacetera, Pope & Sydnor (2012), American Economic Review 102(5). https://www.nber.org/papers/w17030 | abstract and working-paper summary (not used in the script) |
| "Prices just under a round number tend to sell more than one cent should explain" | the studies above, stated as a hedge ("studies find", "tend to") | secondary; no lift figure given |
| The lamp story (4.00 sells none, 3.99 sells a pile) | an invented illustration, not data | n/a |

## Not claimed, on purpose
- No percentage lift in sales. The studies differ and the effect is not guaranteed.
- No claim that every nine-ending raises sales, or that it works on every shopper.
- No claim about why retailers began using .99 (the history is disputed).
- The "SOLD 99" board is a joke, not a result.
