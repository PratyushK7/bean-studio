# Sources: weekend-flies-fast

| Claim in the script | Source | Checked how far |
|---|---|---|
| A weekend is 48 hours (Saturday and Sunday) | arithmetic | n/a |
| A Monday meeting and a weekend are made of the same minutes; the clock does not slow | arithmetic / physics of clocks | n/a |
| "Scientists think" the brain estimates time by how much attention goes to time; paying more attention makes an interval feel longer, less attention makes it feel shorter | Zakay & Block (1995), attentional gate model: https://www.montana.edu/rblock/documents/papers/ZakayBlock1995.pdf ; Zakay (2014), Frontiers in Psychology: https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00917/full | search summaries of the papers only (not full text); the review notes the internal-clock framework is debated, so the script hedges with "scientists think" |
| Engaging or enjoyable activity makes time seem to pass faster, boredom slows it | Sackett, Meyvis, Nelson, Converse & Sackett (2010), Psychological Science 21(1), 111-117, DOI 10.1177/0956797609354832: https://core.ac.uk/download/217156896.pdf | abstract and secondary reports only |

## Not claimed, on purpose
- That the weekend is objectively shorter, or that time literally speeds up.
- Any number of seconds, minutes or percentage for the effect.
- That one brain region or one clock does the timing (the research is still debated).
- That people looking back on a week judge it the same way (retrospective timing works differently; left out to keep one mechanism).
- Hook honesty: the video says time flies "when you're having fun" is "not quite it", then that time flies when you stop counting and "fun just helps you forget". It does not say fun has no effect (Sackett et al. 2010 found enjoyable tasks feel shorter); it says attention to the clock is the likelier mechanism ("scientists think").
