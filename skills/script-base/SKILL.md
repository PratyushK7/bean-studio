# Script base: how every script is written

The owner's rule (2026-11-20): this method is the base of every video script. The channel skills add research, fact
rules, language and production on top. Where a channel skill's structure rules conflict with this file, this file wins.
The course text is kept in `references/source-course.txt`. Re-read the hook, body and editing parts when unsure.

## What a good script is

YouTube pushes what viewers are **satisfied** by. They leave feeling entertained, having learned something, or moved;
the best videos do all three. A script is good when:
1. **The language is simple.** A twelve-year-old follows every sentence, with no jargon left unexplained.
2. **It gives the viewer exactly what the title promised**, from the first 30 seconds to the end.
3. **You would pay ₹1,000 to watch it in a cinema.** (The course says $100: would you pay to watch your own video?)
4. **You would watch it yourself, to the end.**

Script it in full, word for word. A full script is easier to record or voice, easier to edit, and it performs better.

## 1. Know the type of video

Story video, documentary, video sales letter, or challenge. Ours:
- **Jad Tak:** documentary told as a story.
- **Decoding Vyapaar:** documentary or explainer told as a story.
- **The Bean With Bad Ideas:** an educational story.

The type decides the structure: a story needs a character who wants something, and an explainer needs points.

## 2. The title comes first

Fix the title before writing a single line. The title sets the expectation, and the script exists to meet it. Ideas
come from outliers, videos that beat their channel's normal views:
- small creators with a clear outlier
- big creators in the niche
- adjacent niches
- broad niches

Write many ideas, cut them to ten, and pick one.

**Then list the questions the title and thumbnail put in the viewer's head.** Write up to ten, then keep the four or five
that burn most. Example: "Was Neem Karoli Baba really Hanuman?" raises:
- Who was he?
- Why do people say he was Hanuman?
- What did he himself say?
- What happened that made people believe it?
- So was he, or not?

The hook and introduction must touch every one of these, and the body must answer them. Also note:
- **Who** is watching, and the words they use.
- What they will **doubt**.
- What **changes** for them by the end.
- What is **at stake**.

## 3. Research

- **Read the comments** of the top videos on the subject: what people loved, argued about, asked for, or complained
  about (for example "too long", "skip to 13:40"). That gap is your angle.
- **Study the top creators' most popular videos** one by one: the format, the emotion of the title and thumbnail, why
  the hook worked, why the story worked, why people stayed to the end. Copy what works and do it better.
- Stop when you have what you need. **A script that wanders off its point is a research problem:** the writer is
  filling a gap with whatever they have.

## 4. The hook: the first 30 seconds

The hook's two jobs: **prove the click was right**, and **make the viewer curious enough to stay**. It must be clear
and engaging. Avoid:
- **jargon**
- **over-explaining**, which oversells the video
- **credentials** the viewer doesn't need

**The first line (about 5 seconds).** Choose one of these ten types:
1. A question.
2. A shocking statement.
3. A story ("I went from … to …").
4. A preview of what happens.
5. A personal connection.
6. A statistic or fact.
7. A challenge.
8. A quotation.
9. A metaphor.
10. Proof.

A bold first line must be backed up by the next one or two lines. The first line is the hardest to write; the rest
flow from it.

**The introduction** (everything after the first line) does three things:
- gives context
- sets the stakes
- names the payoff: what the viewer gets by watching

It answers the burning questions from step 2.

Every line in the introduction must earn the next line. If it doesn't add context or pull the viewer forward, cut it.
Spend more time here than anywhere else.

When the viewer needs a frame before the main content makes sense, a longer context section is fine: the course uses
about 250 words, 1 to 2 minutes. It must still be about the title.

## 5. The body

- **Points, in order of importance:** break the body into sub-points. Present each next point as more important than
  the last, so the viewer keeps waiting for the next one.
- **Open loops between points:** don't announce "point two is …". Build curiosity first ("the next step is the one
  everyone skips, and it's the one that drives the most results"). Explain why it matters, and only then name it.
  The pattern is: open loop → point → open loop → point.
- **Bullets first:** write each point as bullets before the full sentences, or the logic gets muddy.
- **Ask what the viewer would ask** and answer it. For an explainer point:
  - **Why** does it matter?
  - What is the **proof**?
  - Who credible says so?
  - **How** do I do it?

  For a story point in a documentary:
  - What **happened**, told as a scene?
  - **Why** does it matter to the title's question?
  - What does it **prove**, and what doesn't it prove?
- **Show, don't tell:** a clip, a picture, a before-and-after or a side-by-side comparison beats a description.
  Write the visual into the script.
- **Contrast:** two camps (people who do X and people who do Y), or a before state and an after state. Contrast creates
  tension.
- **Stay on the point:** if a section drifts to something the title didn't promise, cut it, however good it is.

## 6. Storytelling

- **Somebody wanted, but, so, then.** A character wants something, an obstacle stops them, they act, and something
  happens. Write this sentence for every anecdote, and **one for the whole video**. If the whole video has no
  "somebody" and no "wanted", it's a list, not a story.
- **The hero's journey** is the long form of the same thing: a call, a threshold, a mentor, trials, a fall, a change,
  a return. Build tension to a climax, then resolve it.
- **Start with the end in mind.** Decide the emotion the viewer should leave with (wonder, sadness, surprise, hope), then
  work backwards so everything leads there.
- **Simplify to the essence.** Say what the video is really saying in one sentence. Strip out everything that distracts
  from it. The simpler the story, the better.
- **Contrast between states:** where the character was, where they want to be, and why it's hard.

## 7. The outro and the call to action

- **One call to action only.** More than one confuses the viewer.
- **Point to another video** with **hook, curiosity, action**: catch the viewer's attention once more just before the
  end, open a knowledge gap the next video fills, then tell them to watch it. The course says this lifts end-screen
  clicks from about 5% to about 15%.
- **No "like and subscribe"** unless there is no other video to point to (a channel's first video). A good video earns
  likes on its own.
- Pitch a product only after a very long, high-value video (40 minutes or more).
- **Don't recap the points.** Give a reason to act.

## 8. Editing (every script, one or two rounds)

The first draft is always bad, and that's fine. The edit is where the script becomes good.
1. **Let it rest:** edit it later, with fresh eyes.
2. **Comment first, edit after:** go line by line and write what's wrong with each line, then fix. Switching between
   finding and fixing is confusing.
3. **Read it aloud, and write like you talk.** If a sentence is hard to say, split it. If no one says a word in
   conversation, replace it.
4. **Grammar and spelling pass.**
5. **Logical flow:** sentence one leads to sentence two, and point one leads to point two. A sudden topic with no
   bridge is a fail.
6. **Line-by-line questions:**
   - Is it clear?
   - Is it simple?
   - Is it true?
   - Is it on the point?
   - Does it link to the line before?
7. **Common fixes:**
   - Cut adverbs that add nothing.
   - Turn stacked questions into statements.
   - Drop slang the audience won't know.
   - Never claim the audience thinks something they don't.
   - Don't exaggerate past what they'll recognise.
8. If nearly every line has a problem, **rewrite from scratch** instead of patching.

## The framework sheet (fill it in for every script)

```
Title:
Questions the title raises (4 to 5):
Viewer: who / their words / their doubts / what changes for them / the stakes
One-sentence essence:
Somebody wanted ___, but ___, so ___, then ___.
Ending emotion:
Hook: first line (type) + backing lines + context, stakes, payoff (answers the questions)
Body, for each point (in rising importance):
  open loop -> why / what / how (or: the scene / why it matters / what it proves) -> example, evidence, visual -> payoff
Call to action: the point that links to this video -> the curiosity gap -> watch this next video
Edit: rested, commented, read aloud, flow checked, grammar checked
```

## The 10-point review (rate any script out of 10)

Score each item 1 (done), 0.5 (partly) or 0 (missing), and give evidence (line numbers) for each.

1. **The title is delivered:** the script gives exactly what the title promises, start to end.
2. **The hook proves the click within 30 seconds:** a strong first line, backed by the next lines.
3. **The introduction answers the title's burning questions** and sets context, stakes and payoff.
4. **Simple, spoken language:** a twelve-year-old follows it, and it is written like someone talks, not like a book.
5. **One essence:** every section serves the title, with no detours.
6. **The body escalates:** each point matters more than the last, joined by open loops.
7. **Every point answers why, what and how** (or scene, why it matters, what it proves) with evidence, shown not told.
8. **It is a story:** someone wants something, against an obstacle, with contrast between states. Somebody, wanted,
   but, so, then holds for the whole video.
9. **The ending was planned first:** the final emotion lands, and there is one call to action, made with hook,
   curiosity, action.
10. **It is edited:** logical flow line to line, every line earns its place, it reads aloud cleanly, and it passes
    "would I pay to watch this?".

A script below 8 is rewritten, not patched.
