# Long-form #1: "6 Bugs in Your Brain That Cost You Money"

Researched 4 Nov 2026. A funny animated explainer on an **abstract** subject, told by **different beans**: each bug is a bean.
About 8 minutes, landscape 16:9. Title linted with yt-package: 40 characters, 100/100, fits the mobile cut. Thumbnail words: **BUG FOUND**.

The channel is "The Bean With Bad Ideas": the name is a brand, not a limit. This video is about money psychology, not bad ideas.

## Why this topic

**Genre-agnostic data first.** In the long-video study (691 videos, 20 explainer channels) genre did not predict outliers; format did:
- funny animated everyday-life explainers: TheOdd1sOut "Junk Food" 91M views (6.5x its median), "Scams That Should be Illegal" 75M (5.4x)
- 5 to 12 minute explainers on abstract, useful subjects: TED-Ed "4 ways to fix your attention span" (11.3x), AsapSCIENCE on sleep (9.0x), MinuteEarth "ALL THE X, EXPLAINED" (6.5x)

**Saturation check across genres** (YouTube search, 4 Nov, yt-dlp). Rejected as saturated: procrastination (TED-Ed 11M, Domics 9M), inflation (Johnny Harris 2.3M, The Economist 3.4M), habits (Kurzgesagt 15.9M), attention span (TEDx 20M, TED-Ed 4.5M), compound interest (several 1 to 3M). Open:
- "sunk cost fallacy explained": top result 718k, median 7.7k, nothing polished and animated
- "money mistakes your brain makes behavioral economics explained": 22 results, none over 500k; the top two are university lectures of 78 and 47 minutes; the rest are tiny channels (median 17 views)
- "mental accounting anchoring sunk cost explained animated": median 8 views

**Adjacent demand is large**, so the audience exists: Practical Psychology "12 Cognitive Biases Explained" 2.8M; SciShow Psych "How Restaurants Use Psychology to Make You Spend More Money" 1.7M; TED-Ed "The psychology behind irrational decisions" 2.4M; Nischa "Money Habits Keeping You Poor" 11.2M.

**Why it fits the channel.** It is an explainer a viewer can use and repeat. The subject is abstract, which is the point: a bug is a bean. It is funny by construction (a bean stuck in a chair because he already paid for the ticket). It is evergreen (no season), and relevant to the US and India (401(k) and a monthly SIP are both "defaults").

**Honest caveats**
- Search interest in "cognitive bias" is high but the money angle is competitive on text-heavy channels; the gap is *animated, funny, and honest about which results are contested*.
- Behavioural economics has had replication problems. This video uses only effects with long, public records, flags the one that is contested (loss aversion), and leaves out popular but shaky ones (see "Not used").
- This is a sample of search results, not full demand data.

## The six bugs (each is a bean), with the check on every claim

The host is the red **Debugger Bean** (hard hat, clipboard of bug reports). Each bug is a bean with small antennae and its own colour, shape and prop. A fix ("patch") ends each segment.

| # | Bug bean | The claim | Source | Check |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Hook | Your brain has bugs. They have names, they cost money, here are six with the studies and a one-line patch for each. | | |
| 1 | **Sunk Cost Bean** (paper wide bean glued to a seat, holding a ticket stub) | In a field experiment at the Ohio University Theater, people were randomly sold a season ticket at full price or at a discount. Full-price buyers attended more plays over the next months, although the money was gone either way. Taking economics courses did not remove the effect. **Patch:** ask only what happens *from here*. | Arkes and Blumer, *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* 35:124-140 (1985) | Abstract confirmed. **Do not quote attendance numbers** (secondary sources disagree) |
| 2 | **Anchor Bean** (tall blue bean wearing a small anchor) | A wheel of fortune was rigged to stop at 10 or 65, then people estimated the share of African countries in the UN. Estimates followed the number: roughly 25 versus roughly 45. **Patch:** write your own number before you see theirs. | Tversky and Kahneman, *Science* 185:1124-1131 (1974) | Direction confirmed; figures are from secondary sources and **mean vs median is unconfirmed**, so say "roughly" |
| 3 | **Mental-Accounting Bean** (yellow box bean with labelled envelopes) | You have lost a $10 theatre ticket, or a $10 bill. Would you still buy a ticket? Lost ticket: 46% yes. Lost cash: 88% yes. Same $10. **Patch:** ask whether this money is different, or just in a different jar. | Kahneman and Tversky, early 1980s (sources say 1981, 1983 or 1984; about 200 people per version) | Figures consistent across sources; **confirm year and wording in the original** |
| 4 | **Present Bean vs Future Bean** (a huge loud red ball vs a tiny blue bean far away, in a tug of war) | Health-club records for 7,752 members: people on flat-fee contracts of over $70 a month went about 4.3 times a month, over $17 a visit, when a 10-visit pass cost $10 a visit. **Patch:** buy for the person you actually are, and automate what you want to do. | DellaVigna and Malmendier, "Paying Not to Go to the Gym", *American Economic Review* 96(3):694-719 (2006) | Abstract and summaries agree. **Do not quote the total loss ($600 or $700; sources differ)** |
| 5 | **Default Bean** (paper pear bean on a sofa, never presses the button) | When employers switched 401(k) retirement plans to automatic enrolment, participation rose by tens of percentage points; many people also stayed at the low default savings rate. **Patch:** set your own defaults on purpose (savings on payday, a monthly SIP, cancel auto-renew). | Madrian and Shea, *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 116(4):1149-1187 (2001); Choi, Laibson, Madrian and Metrick | Direction confirmed; **the exact "37% to 86%" is unverified, so do not use it** |
| 6 | **Loss Bean** (red ball bean, permanently worried, with a tiny violin) | Losses sting more than equal gains, famously by about two to one. But this is the bug with a bug: later work argues the effect is conditional, and estimates range from below 1 to well above 2. **Patch:** judge the final outcome, not the swing. | Kahneman and Tversky 1979; Gal and Rucker 2018 ("The loss of loss aversion"); replies by Simonson and Kivetz, Mrkva and colleagues 2020 | Debate confirmed in summaries; **read both sides before recording** |
| 7 | Close | Bugs don't go away, you just learn to spot them. Debugger Bean shuts the laptop. Callback to the first bean's ticket stub. | | |

### Not used, on purpose
- **Save More Tomorrow** (Thaler and Benartzi 2004): the headline numbers I found conflict (one summary says 4.4% to 10.6% at a firm, other pages say 3.5% to 13.6% over 40 months). Verify in the paper or skip.
- **Madrian and Shea "37% to 86%"**: could not be confirmed.
- **Decoy and "free" pricing studies by Dan Ariely**: do not use until there is an independent replication to cite.
- **Ego depletion, power posing, priming results**: failed replications.
- **Endowment-effect mug numbers**: contested.
- **"The brain evolved on the savannah" explanations**: untestable, skip.

## Funny, by design

- A running gag: every bean arrives with a **bug report stamp** (NEW, FILED, WON'T FIX). Loss Bean gets "DISPUTED".
- Sunk Cost Bean keeps watching a terrible film because he paid, eventually in a chair that has turned into a fossil.
- Present Bean and Future Bean text each other and never reply in time.
- The gym is a bean paying $17 a visit to a treadmill that does not know his name.
- Deadpan, one joke every 10 to 15 seconds, in the beans' reactions and the last line of each segment. Never at the expense of the facts.
