## Why this topic (the evidence)

**1. Proven demand next door.** Long videos on mosquitoes and bites beat their own channels' medians:
- TED-Ed, "Why mosquitoes bite some people more than others": 2.2M views
- SciShow, "How to Become Immune to Mosquito Bites": 1.2M views
- MinuteEarth, "Why Are Some People Mosquito Magnets?": 3.0M views, 8.1x its channel median (channel study, 3 Nov)

**2. A real gap on the exact angle.** Searching YouTube for "mosquito repellent myths science" (25 results, yt-dlp, 4 Nov): the best direct result is a 54k-view garden channel ("I Tested 11 Mosquito Plants"), then 22k, 8.6k and 7.5k. Median results: 471 views. Nobody has made a polished, animated, ranked "what doesn't work and why" explainer that also tells you what does.

**3. It is our format.** The channel is "bad ideas, explained with real science". Folk remedies are literally bad ideas. The ranked-list and myth-test formats are already two of our 13 types, and they ranked well in the study (Mark Rober "Are Bananas Actually Slippery?" 10.6x, SciShow "Ranking Ridiculous Exoplanets" 13.3x on Shorts).

**4. It reuses what exists.** Moz the mosquito, the blue bean and the red host from the mosquito Short; the spin-off Shorts below.

**5. The length fits.** In the long-form data the explainer outliers are 5 to 12 minutes (MinuteEarth "ALL THE CATS, EXPLAINED" 12 min, 6.8x; "Why Don't We Use Mothballs Anymore?" 11 min, 6.5x; TED-Ed 4 to 5 min at 8 to 11x). The median long video in the study is 14 minutes, but the winners for small animated channels are shorter.

Rejected, with reasons:
- *Lawn Chair Larry / balloons long-form:* the story is saturated (10.9M, 1.1M, 0.8M, 0.6M views for the top results). Our Short already covers our angle.
- *"Body myths" compilation (gum, nails, knuckles):* crowded (AsapSCIENCE, Life Noggin), and no single search intent.

**Honest caveats**
- Search interest is seasonal in the US (peaks May to September) and it is November. The video is evergreen and India has year-round mosquito concern, but expect a slower first month in the US. Re-share in spring.
- Search results are a sample, not full demand data. The 471-view median could also mean the query is rarely typed; the adjacent queries (millions of views) say the topic is wanted.

## The script, ranked from "does nothing" to "kind of works"

Voice: calm, dry, no hype. Every figure below was found in a source; the **check** column says how well it was verified in this research.

| 1 | Why you | Some people are consistently far more attractive to mosquitoes. In a 2022 study the most attractive person drew about 100x the mosquitoes of the least. | 3 | Vitamin B1 | A 2022 review of 104 papers found no evidence thiamine repels insects at any dose; early "it works" reports were anecdotal or used bite symptoms as a proxy. | 4 | Ultrasonic repellers and phone apps | A Cochrane review of 10 field trials found electronic repellents have no effect on bites. | Enayati, Hemingway and Garner, Cochrane CD005434 (2007) | Abstract. Old review, 2009 search date |

| 7 | "Mosquito plants" | Citrosa (Pelargonium citrosum) did not reduce landings in a 1994 Florida test or a 1996 Guelph test; DEET cut bites by over 90% for up to 8 hours in the same work. | 8 | Citronella candles | The least-bad bad idea: partial, short-range. Reductions of 42% (1996 Ontario, 3% candle), 35% fewer mosquitoes trapped at 1 m (2008 JAMCA), and 14% (a 2009 study). | 9 | What works: repellent | EPA-registered ingredients (DEET, picaridin, IR3535, oil of lemon eucalyptus/PMD) are the ones to use. In the 2002 NEJM test a 23.8% DEET product gave about 5 hours of complete protection (301.5 minutes); most plant-based products under 20 minutes; a soybean-oil product about 95 minutes. | CDC and EPA guidance; Fradin and Day 2002 | NEJM figures from a summary; **check current CDC page before quoting durations** |
| 10 | What works: a fan | Wind helps mainly by diluting the carbon dioxide trail mosquitoes follow, not by out-flying them (2003 *J. Medical Entomology* 40:607). Do **not** quote the "74/75/70%" figures: those were a fan pushing air through a DEET screen, not a fan alone. Consumer Reports' "45 to 65% fewer landings" is a secondary claim; leave it out unless verified. | PubMed 14596273 (2003), PubMed 12495196 (2002) | **Needs the exact fan-alone number from the paper, or none** |

**Corrections baked into the beats above (found while researching):**
1. The widely repeated "fan cuts bites 74%" belongs to a DEET-screen experiment. Not used.
2. Candle numbers range from 14% to 42% depending on the study, so the script must say "partial" and not give one number.
3. The wristband result is from one lab study of the products tested in 2002. Say "in that test".
4. Pure essential oils are not EPA-registered; only specific products with oil of lemon eucalyptus/PMD are.
5. Do not claim mosquitoes "prefer blood type O" as settled: the 2004 landing result is for one species (Aedes albopictus), and the "sweet blood" myth had no source in this research. Leave it out, or mention as "one small study".
